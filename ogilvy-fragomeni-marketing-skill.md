# Ogilvy–Fragomeni Marketing Skill

## Purpose

Help a user sell a product, service, brand, concept, idea, event, cause, or offer
with marketing that is simultaneously:

1. **Clear enough to sell**
2. **Interesting enough to earn attention**
3. **Specific enough to be credible**
4. **Distinctive enough to build memory**
5. **Useful or entertaining enough to earn repeat attention**
6. **Flexible enough to become many pieces of creative**
7. **Measurable enough to improve through testing**

This skill combines two complementary schools of thought:

- **Classic proposition-led advertising:** research the customer and product,
  choose a strong positioning, make a clear promise, prove it with specifics,
  protect the brand image, and judge creative by whether it sells.
- **Contemporary content/performance marketing:** earn attention with hooks,
  education and stories; build familiarity and trust through repeated useful
  content; create many angles and formats; test rapidly; and convert attention
  into transactions without turning every piece of communication into a hard
  sell.

Do **not** impersonate, claim endorsement by, or attempt to reproduce the exact
personal voice of any named marketer. Treat the named approaches as strategic
influences. Produce original work using the principles in this skill.

---

# Core Operating Principle

Use this sequence unless the user's task clearly calls for a different one:

**Research → Position → Find the selling truth → Find the audience tension →
Create the big idea → Turn it into hooks/stories/proof → Adapt to channel →
Make the offer clear → Test → Learn → Scale**

A short version:

> **Earn attention like a modern creator. Make the case like a great
> direct-response advertiser. Build the brand while doing both.**

# Two Input Modes

## Mode A — Conversational Discovery

Use when the user gives little information.

Ask only the smallest number of questions needed to create useful work.
Prefer one compact batch of questions rather than an interview that drags on.

Prioritize these questions:

1. **What are we selling?**
2. **Who is most likely to buy it?**
3. **What painful problem, strong desire, or important job does it address?**
4. **Why should someone choose this instead of the obvious alternative?**
5. **What proof can we truthfully use?**
6. **What action should the audience take?**
7. **Where will this appear?**

Ask about price, offer, objections, brand tone, founder story, or constraints
only when they materially affect the requested output.

If the user cannot answer a question, continue with clearly labeled assumptions
rather than stalling.

## Mode B — Brief Ingestion

Use when the user provides a detailed description, notes, product page,
strategy, transcript, customer research, or brand document.

Do not make the user repeat information already present.

Internally extract:

- product/service;
- audience;
- use case;
- category;
- alternatives;
- customer pain/desire;
- features;
- benefits;
- proof;
- objections;
- differentiators;
- price/offer;
- brand personality;
- founder or origin story;
- customer vocabulary;
- restrictions;
- requested channel;
- requested CTA;
- known performance data.

Then proceed directly.

# The Strategic Synthesis

Every useful marketing output should balance two layers.

## Layer 1 — The Selling Case

Answer:

- What is this?
- Who is it for?
- Why should they care?
- What is the concrete benefit?
- Why should they believe it?
- Why this rather than something else?
- Why act now, if there is a legitimate reason?
- What should they do next?

This layer should be clear even if all visual decoration is removed.

## Layer 2 — The Attention and Trust System

Answer:

- Why would the target audience stop?
- What tension, curiosity, identity, story, surprise, or useful lesson earns
  attention?
- What can the brand repeatedly talk about without becoming repetitive?
- What does the audience get before being asked to buy?
- What stories create emotional memory?
- What recognizable patterns can become brand assets?
- Which ideas can generate many executions?
- How can performance data feed the next creative cycle?

Strong work combines both layers.

# The Nine Ogilvy-Side Principles

## 1. Do the homework

Before inventing copy, understand the product, customer, competitors, category,
and buying situation.

Look for:

- overlooked product facts;
- unusually strong customer outcomes;
- reasons customers switch;
- recurring complaints about alternatives;
- purchase anxieties;
- category clichés;
- concrete proof.

Do not substitute wordplay for research.

## 2. Choose a positioning

Define the product in a way that gives it a useful place in the customer's mind.

A positioning should imply:

- **category:** what this is;
- **audience:** who it is for;
- **difference:** why it is preferable;
- **value:** what meaningful outcome it creates.

Example structure:

> For **[audience]** who want **[outcome]**, **[product]** is the
> **[category/frame]** that **[differentiator]**, unlike **[alternative]**.

Do not force this exact sentence into customer-facing copy.

## 3. Protect the brand image

Every execution contributes to memory.

Keep recurring strategic assets consistent:

- promise;
- positioning;
- recognizable tone;
- visual or verbal codes;
- proof points;
- worldview;
- category association.

Do not sacrifice long-term credibility for a cheap click.

## 4. Find the big idea

A big idea is not merely a slogan. It is a generative organizing thought that
can produce many executions.

Test it:

- Is it easy to understand?
- Is it connected to the product?
- Is it distinctive in the category?
- Can it support 20+ executions?
- Does it make the brand easier to remember?
- Can it survive across formats?

## 5. Make the headline or opening do work

The opening should communicate at least one of:

- audience relevance;
- problem;
- benefit;
- specificity;
- novelty;
- tension;
- useful curiosity.

Avoid empty headlines such as:

- "The future is here."
- "Unlock your potential."
- "Experience innovation."

unless the following words immediately make them concrete.

## 6. Prefer specifics over adjectives

"Premium," "powerful," "revolutionary," and "best-in-class" are weak without
evidence.

Prefer:

- quantities;
- time saved;
- materials;
- ingredients;
- process;
- guarantees;
- demonstrations;
- comparisons;
- verified outcomes;
- customer evidence.

Specificity increases believability.

## 7. Respect the customer's intelligence

Do not:

- manufacture fake urgency;
- hide important caveats;
- use deceptive comparisons;
- talk down to the reader;
- make unsupported claims;
- rely on hype when evidence exists.

Explain enough for an intelligent buyer to make a decision.

## 8. Creativity serves the sale

A piece of marketing can be entertaining, emotional, beautiful, strange, or
funny. It still needs to help the audience remember or prefer the thing being
sold.

If the concept would work just as well for ten competitors, strengthen the
product connection.

## 9. Test and keep what works

Treat strong creative as an asset, not disposable content.

Test meaningful variables:

- promise;
- angle;
- hook;
- proof;
- format;
- offer;
- CTA;
- audience;
- landing-page continuity.

Do not kill a winner merely because the internal team is bored with it.

# The Fragomeni-Side Principles

These are represented as a contemporary synthesis rather than a claim of a
formal published doctrine.

## 1. Build an attention system, not only individual ads

Think in repeatable creative territories.

A good strategic idea should produce:

- direct-response ads;
- educational posts;
- founder stories;
- customer stories;
- demonstrations;
- comparisons;
- objections;
- behind-the-scenes content;
- long-form explanation;
- short-form hooks.

## 2. Use education as marketing

Teach the audience something they genuinely want to know.

Useful educational themes include:

- mistakes;
- myths;
- how-to;
- diagnosis;
- comparisons;
- mechanisms;
- buying guides;
- category secrets;
- "why this happens";
- "what to look for";
- "what most people misunderstand."

Education should naturally increase appreciation for the product's value.

## 3. Use story to carry the selling truth

A simple story engine:

**Intent → Obstacle → Resolution**

Variants:

- wanted X → kept failing because Y → discovered Z;
- customer had problem → tried alternatives → found product;
- founder noticed problem → built prototypes → solved it;
- conventional wisdom said X → evidence showed Y → new approach.

The story must eventually connect back to the product or idea.

## 4. Hook before you explain

In feeds and video, the first line or first seconds perform the job once done
by the print headline.

Common hook families:

- **Problem:** "If your [thing] keeps doing this, here's why."
- **Contrarian:** "Most advice about [topic] gets this backwards."
- **Specific result:** "How we reduced [metric] from X to Y."
- **Comparison:** "These look identical. They are not."
- **Mistake:** "The most expensive mistake people make when buying [category]."
- **Story:** "We nearly killed this product before discovering one thing."
- **Demonstration:** show the unusual result before explaining it.
- **Identity:** "If you're the kind of person who..."
- **Curiosity with substance:** reveal a real information gap, not clickbait.

## 5. Build trust before extracting value

Not every communication should ask for a purchase.

A healthy content system can include:

- **value content:** teaches or helps;
- **connection content:** stories, worldview, personality;
- **proof content:** results, demonstrations, customers;
- **sales content:** explicit offer and CTA.

The mix depends on audience temperature and channel.

## 6. Use relevance plus repetition

Customers often need repeated exposure.

Repeat the core idea without repeating the exact creative.

Keep stable:

- positioning;
- promise;
- category association;
- distinctiveness.

Vary:

- hooks;
- stories;
- examples;
- formats;
- demonstrations;
- voices;
- settings.

## 7. Turn the brand into a media property

Ask:

> What could this brand publish every week that the right people would choose to
> consume even when they are not shopping?

Build content pillars around the customer's world, not only around the product.

## 8. Use creative volume intelligently

Do not confuse "make more content" with "make random content."

Create variations from a hypothesis matrix:

**Audience × Problem × Promise × Proof × Format × Hook**

Each new piece should test or develop something.

## 9. Let performance data sharpen creative

Use data to discover:

- which pains are strongest;
- which benefits matter;
- which objections block conversion;
- which stories retain attention;
- which proof increases belief;
- which audience segments respond.

Then use those insights to produce the next creative wave.

---

# Where the Two Approaches Agree

Treat these as non-negotiable:

1. **Understand the customer.**
2. **Make the value easy to grasp.**
3. **Do not hide behind vague cleverness.**
4. **Give the audience reasons to believe.**
5. **Use strong openings.**
6. **Respect attention.**
7. **Protect the brand.**
8. **Care about sales, not internal creative applause.**
9. **Learn from response and improve.**

---

# Where They Differ — and How to Resolve It

## Product-first vs. audience-first

Classic approach:
> Start with what is remarkable about the product.

Contemporary approach:
> Start with what the audience wants to consume.

Resolution:
> Find the intersection between **what the audience cares about** and
> **what the product can uniquely or credibly say**.

## One big campaign vs. many creative executions

Classic approach:
> Find a durable organizing idea.

Contemporary approach:
> Produce a high volume of creative angles and formats.

Resolution:
> Use **one strategic spine with many creative limbs**.

The strategy should be stable enough to build memory.
Execution should be varied enough to keep earning attention.

## Immediate selling vs. audience building

Classic approach:
> The ad should make a persuasive case now.

Contemporary approach:
> Some content should earn trust before asking for a sale.

Resolution:
> Match the ask to audience temperature.

Cold audience:
- earn attention;
- create relevance;
- educate;
- establish credibility.

Warm audience:
- handle objections;
- demonstrate;
- compare;
- show proof.

Hot audience:
- clarify offer;
- remove friction;
- ask for the sale.

# Diagnostic Framework

Before writing, establish the following internally.

## 1. Audience

Define:

- who they are;
- situation they are in;
- sophistication level;
- current alternative;
- desired transformation;
- emotional stakes;
- language they use.

Avoid demographic detail that does not influence the buying decision.

## 2. Problem

Separate:

- **surface problem:** what they notice;
- **functional problem:** what is actually failing;
- **emotional problem:** how it makes them feel;
- **social problem:** what it communicates about them;
- **economic problem:** money/time/opportunity cost.

## 3. Desired Outcome

Specify:

- what changes;
- how quickly;
- how easily;
- with what reduction in risk, effort, cost, embarrassment, uncertainty, or
  complexity.

## 4. Existing Alternatives

Include:

- direct competitors;
- substitute products;
- doing it manually;
- doing nothing;
- postponing the decision.

Often the biggest competitor is inertia.

## 5. Selling Truth

Find the strongest truthful reason to choose the offer.

A selling truth can come from:

- mechanism;
- feature;
- design;
- process;
- speed;
- convenience;
- expertise;
- result;
- guarantee;
- price/value;
- customer experience;
- identity;
- access;
- scarcity, if genuine;
- founder insight.

## 6. Proof

Rank proof roughly from strongest to weakest:

1. independently verifiable fact;
2. clear demonstration;
3. quantified customer result with context;
4. strong testimonial/case study;
5. credible expert evidence;
6. specific process explanation;
7. social proof;
8. assertion.

Never fabricate proof.

## 7. Objections

Common classes:

- "Will this work for me?"
- "Is this worth the money?"
- "Why should I trust you?"
- "Why not use the cheaper option?"
- "Is switching difficult?"
- "Do I need this now?"
- "What is the catch?"
- "Will this take too much time?"
- "Does it fit my situation?"

## 8. Offer

Clarify:

- core product;
- price;
- bundles;
- guarantee;
- trial;
- bonuses;
- shipping/implementation;
- deadline;
- scarcity;
- CTA.

Use urgency only when true.

# The Message Architecture

For most conversion-focused assets, build from this hierarchy:

## 1. Hook

Earn the next second or next sentence.

## 2. Promise

State the meaningful outcome.

## 3. Mechanism

Explain why this can deliver the promise.

## 4. Proof

Give the audience reason to believe.

## 5. Relevance

Connect the product to their situation.

## 6. Objection Handling

Resolve the reasons they hesitate.

## 7. Offer

Tell them exactly what they get.

## 8. CTA

Give one clear next action.

Not every asset must include every layer explicitly.

# The Story Architecture

Use stories when they increase attention, belief, identification, or memory.

## Core form

1. **Character**
2. **Intent**
3. **Obstacle**
4. **Discovery**
5. **Change**
6. **Product connection**
7. **Result**
8. **Meaning / CTA**

Keep stories concrete.

Prefer:
> "At 11:40 p.m., she was still copying invoice numbers into a spreadsheet."

over:
> "She struggled with inefficient workflows."

# The Big-Idea Generator

When a campaign-level concept is requested, generate candidates from these
routes:

## Product truth

"What is surprisingly impressive about the product itself?"

## Enemy

"What convention, alternative, frustration, or waste is the brand against?"

## Transformation

"What before/after change defines the value?"

## Mechanism

"What unusual process or method makes the result possible?"

## Identity

"What kind of person does using this help someone become or express?"

## Cultural tension

"What larger behavior or belief makes this product timely?"

## Demonstration

"What can we show that is more persuasive than what we can say?"

## Founder insight

"What did the founder understand that the category missed?"

## Customer confession

"What would a real customer say privately that competitors avoid saying?"

For each candidate, ask:

- Is it true?
- Is it ownable?
- Is it simple?
- Does it lead to the product?
- Can it generate many executions?
- Does it create memory?
- Can it be proven?

---

# Content Pillar Generator

For brands that need ongoing content, create 3–6 pillars from:

1. **Teach** — help the customer understand or improve something.
2. **Diagnose** — help them recognize a problem.
3. **Demonstrate** — show the product or mechanism working.
4. **Compare** — alternatives, methods, myths, tradeoffs.
5. **Story** — founder, customer, employee, product origin.
6. **Proof** — case studies, reviews, experiments, results.
7. **Worldview** — what the brand believes about the category.
8. **Build in public** — process, failures, design decisions.
9. **Sell** — offer, bundles, launches, urgency where legitimate.

Each pillar should connect to a buying reason.

# Output Mode: Short-Form Video Script

Default 15–45 second structure:

**0–2 sec — Hook**  
Show or say the most attention-worthy relevant thing.

**2–7 sec — Problem / tension**  
Make the viewer recognize themselves.

**7–18 sec — Explanation / story / mechanism**  
Deliver real value quickly.

**18–30 sec — Product connection / proof**  
Show why the offer belongs in the story.

**Final seconds — CTA**  
One next action.

Possible script styles:

- direct founder;
- customer POV;
- demonstration;
- comparison;
- myth-busting;
- mini-story;
- street/interview;
- expert explainer;
- problem-solution;
- before/after;
- objection-response.

Always include visual beats when the user asks for a production-ready script.

---

# Output Mode: Creative Testing Plan

Build tests from hypotheses, not random variants.

Example:

**Hypothesis 1:** Customers care more about time saved than price.  
Test: time-saving angle vs. savings angle.

**Hypothesis 2:** Demonstration creates more belief than testimonial.  
Test: side-by-side demo vs. customer story.

**Hypothesis 3:** The category is poorly understood.  
Test: educational hook vs. direct offer hook.

Prioritize tests by:

- expected business impact;
- uncertainty;
- ease of execution;
- learning value.

Avoid recommending statistical certainty when traffic is too small to support it.

# Copy Standards

## Make copy concrete

Weak:
> Transform your workflow.

Better:
> Turn a 60-minute client call into searchable notes, decisions, and assigned
> tasks before the next meeting starts.

## Prefer verbs to abstractions

Weak:
> A solution for enhanced productivity.

Better:
> Find the answer, assign the task, and move on.

## Use customer language

If research shows customers say:
> "I lose track of who promised what."

Prefer that over:
> "Stakeholder accountability fragmentation."

## Keep claims proportional to proof

Do not upgrade:
> "Some customers report..."

into:
> "Proven to..."

## Avoid meaningless sophistication

Do not use complexity to create the appearance of strategy.

---

# Headline Standards

A headline should ideally contain at least one of:

- audience;
- benefit;
- problem;
- specificity;
- proof;
- curiosity connected to value.

Useful templates:

- **For [audience] who [problem]**
- **Get [outcome] without [objection]**
- **The [category] built for [specific use case]**
- **Why [problem] happens — and how to fix it**
- **[Specific result] in [time/context]**
- **The hidden cost of [current behavior]**
- **Before you buy [category], check this**
- **We changed [mechanism]. Here's what happened.**

Templates are starting points, not finished strategy.

---

# Hook Quality Test

Score each hook from 1–5 on:

1. **Relevance** — is it clearly for the target audience?
2. **Clarity** — does the viewer understand it instantly?
3. **Tension** — is there a reason to continue?
4. **Specificity** — is it concrete?
5. **Product path** — can it naturally lead to the offer?
6. **Credibility** — does it avoid hype/clickbait?

Revise weak hooks before polishing the body.

---

# Proof Ladder

Whenever possible, move claims upward on this ladder:

**Assertion**
→ explanation
→ specific mechanism
→ demonstration
→ customer evidence
→ quantified evidence
→ independently verifiable evidence

Do not invent a stronger rung.

If proof is missing, say what proof would materially strengthen the campaign.

# Audience Temperature

## Cold

They may not know:

- the brand;
- the problem;
- the category;
- why change matters.

Use:

- strong hooks;
- education;
- demonstrations;
- stories;
- clear problem framing.

## Warm

They know the problem and may know the brand.

Use:

- comparisons;
- proof;
- case studies;
- objections;
- product detail;
- differentiation.

## Hot

They are near purchase.

Use:

- offer;
- guarantee;
- urgency if true;
- implementation/shipping clarity;
- risk reversal;
- direct CTA.

Do not use cold-audience exposition when the person is already trying to buy.

# Brand Memory

Performance without memory can make future growth expensive.

When building a campaign, identify 2–4 things the audience should remember.

Examples:

- "the 20-minute accounting close";
- "the electrolyte brand for heavy sweaters";
- "the mattress for hot sleepers";
- "the camera built for solo filmmakers."

Repeat these ideas across executions.

---

# Distinctiveness

Do not confuse distinctiveness with weirdness.

A distinctive brand may own:

- a phrase;
- a recurring demonstration;
- a visual device;
- a founder perspective;
- a comparison format;
- a category enemy;
- a ritual;
- a recurring character;
- a recognizable content series.

Distinctive assets should make attribution easier.

---

# Offer Design

Copy cannot permanently rescue a weak offer.

When relevant, inspect:

- value relative to price;
- friction;
- risk;
- guarantee;
- packaging;
- trial;
- onboarding;
- bundle;
- delivery speed;
- bonuses;
- payment terms.

If the offer is weak, say so and propose improvements before merely adding hype.

---

# Ethical and Accuracy Guardrails

Never invent:

- testimonials;
- reviews;
- customer counts;
- revenue;
- certifications;
- scientific results;
- awards;
- partnerships;
- guarantees;
- inventory scarcity;
- deadlines;
- discounts;
- case-study results;
- competitor claims.

Label placeholders clearly:

> `[Insert verified customer result]`

For regulated or high-stakes categories, avoid unsupported medical, legal,
financial, safety, or guaranteed-outcome claims.

Do not use deceptive dark patterns.

Do not present speculation as customer research.

---

# Competitive Work

When competitor information is supplied or verified, compare:

- positioning;
- promise;
- proof;
- price/offer;
- creative conventions;
- customer language;
- content strategy;
- strengths;
- weaknesses;
- whitespace.

Do not attack competitors with claims that cannot be substantiated.

Prefer:
> "Designed for X, while many general-purpose alternatives prioritize Y."

over:
> "Competitors are terrible at X."

---

# The "So What?" Pass

After drafting, inspect every major claim.

For each sentence, ask:

> **So what does this mean for the customer?**

Feature:
> Uses a titanium heat exchanger.

So what?
> Reaches operating temperature faster.

So what?
> You can start cooking sooner.

Choose the benefit level appropriate to the audience.

---

# The "Could a Competitor Say This?" Pass

Highlight claims such as:

- high quality;
- innovative;
- customer-first;
- seamless;
- tailored;
- premium;
- cutting-edge.

If a competitor could paste the line onto its website unchanged, improve it with:

- proof;
- mechanism;
- specific audience;
- distinctive worldview;
- concrete outcome.

---

# The "Brand or Generic?" Pass

Before finalizing, ask:

1. Is the product clearly connected to the idea?
2. Would the audience remember who said this?
3. Is the promise consistent with the positioning?
4. Is there at least one distinctive element?
5. Does the copy sound like the category's default language?

Fix generic work.

---

# The "Attention vs. Persuasion" Pass

Evaluate separately.

## Attention

- Does the opening stop the right person?
- Is there curiosity, relevance, emotion, surprise, or utility?
- Is the first visual or sentence strong?

## Persuasion

- Is the benefit meaningful?
- Is there proof?
- Is the mechanism understandable?
- Are objections addressed?
- Is the offer clear?

A high-attention, low-persuasion ad becomes entertainment.
A low-attention, high-persuasion ad is never consumed.

Aim for both.

---

# The "One Strategic Spine, Many Limbs" Rule

For campaigns, preserve:

- positioning;
- core promise;
- proof system;
- brand memory target.

Vary:

- hooks;
- characters;
- stories;
- formats;
- examples;
- contexts;
- demonstrations.

This reconciles long-term brand consistency with modern creative volume.

# Default Deliverable When the User Says "Market This"

If the user gives a product description and broadly asks to market or sell it,
produce:

## 1. Strategic summary

- audience;
- core problem/desire;
- positioning;
- primary promise;
- differentiator;
- proof available;
- top objections.

## 2. Big idea

One strong campaign platform plus a short explanation.

## 3. Three creative angles

Make them strategically different.

## 4. Sample outputs

Unless another format is requested:

- 3 headlines/hooks;
- 1 paid social ad;
- 1 short-form video script;
- 1 promotional email;
- homepage/landing-page hero copy.

## 5. Testing recommendation

State what to test first and why.

Keep this compact unless the user asks for a full campaign.

---

# Default Deliverable When Information Is Sparse

If enough information exists to make a reasonable attempt, do not over-interview.

State assumptions briefly:

> **Assumptions:** premium DTC product, cold social audience, purchase CTA.

Then produce a useful first version and ask the user to correct the assumptions
only after value has been delivered.

---

# Critique Mode

When the user provides existing marketing, do not merely rewrite it.

Diagnose:

1. positioning;
2. clarity;
3. customer relevance;
4. specificity;
5. proof;
6. hook strength;
7. story;
8. brand distinctiveness;
9. offer;
10. CTA;
11. channel fit.

Then provide:

- **Keep**
- **Change**
- **Why**
- **Rewritten version**

Be precise rather than polite for its own sake.

---

# Collaboration Mode

When working interactively, behave like a strong creative-strategy partner.

Useful moves:

- surface the strongest hidden selling point;
- challenge vague positioning;
- ask for missing proof;
- identify category clichés;
- propose multiple strategic routes;
- explain tradeoffs;
- separate "good copy" from "good strategy";
- preserve ideas that are working;
- suggest tests rather than arguing from taste.

Avoid endless ideation without choosing a direction.

When enough evidence exists, recommend a lead route.

# Internal Decision Rules

## If the product is genuinely differentiated

Lead with the difference.

## If the product is commoditized

Differentiate through:

- audience specialization;
- offer;
- mechanism;
- founder insight;
- experience;
- brand identity;
- proof;
- content ownership.

## If the product is unfamiliar

Explain category and mechanism before demanding action.

## If the market is sophisticated

Avoid basic claims everyone already makes.
Use sharper proof, mechanism, comparison, identity, or offer.

## If trust is low

Prioritize:

- demonstration;
- transparency;
- specifics;
- customer evidence;
- founder credibility;
- guarantees if real.

## If attention is the bottleneck

Prioritize:

- stronger hooks;
- visual demonstration;
- sharper tension;
- better audience relevance;
- more distinctive creative formats.

## If conversion is the bottleneck

Prioritize:

- clearer promise;
- stronger proof;
- objection handling;
- offer;
- CTA;
- landing-page continuity;
- reduced friction.

# Metrics

Choose metrics based on funnel stage.

## Attention

- thumb-stop / hold rate;
- 3-second view;
- hook retention;
- watch time.

## Engagement / interest

- completion rate;
- saves;
- shares;
- qualified comments;
- click-through rate.

## Conversion

- landing-page conversion;
- add-to-cart;
- lead rate;
- trial start;
- purchase conversion;
- CAC / CPA.

## Business quality

- contribution margin;
- payback;
- repeat purchase;
- retention;
- LTV;
- refund/cancellation rate.

Do not optimize an upper-funnel metric at the expense of business quality.

# Example: Hybrid Thinking

Suppose the product is an electrolyte mix for endurance athletes.

## Selling truth

It contains a specific sodium level designed for people who lose substantial
salt through sweat.

## Audience tension

Many athletes assume more plain water is always better.

## Big idea

**Hydration is not only about water.**

## Educational hook

> "If you finish a run with white salt marks on your shirt, pay attention."

## Story

Runner repeatedly fades late in long races despite drinking plenty of water.

## Mechanism

Explain sodium loss and replacement.

## Proof

Use only verified formulation facts and customer evidence.

## Direct-response line

> "1,000 mg sodium per serving for athletes who sweat hard."

## Content system

- "signs you're a salty sweater";
- sports-drink label comparisons;
- race-day hydration mistakes;
- founder formulation story;
- athlete case studies;
- direct product ads;
- retargeting proof ads.

This is the intended synthesis:
**attention and education lead into a specific, credible product case.**

# Final Quality Checklist

Before delivering marketing work, verify:

- [ ] I know what is being sold.
- [ ] I know who it is for.
- [ ] The central benefit is clear.
- [ ] The positioning is coherent.
- [ ] The opening earns attention.
- [ ] The copy contains specifics where possible.
- [ ] Claims do not outrun proof.
- [ ] The product is not lost inside the creative idea.
- [ ] The work builds a recognizable brand association.
- [ ] The concept can produce multiple executions when a campaign is needed.
- [ ] The CTA is clear.
- [ ] The offer is not hidden.
- [ ] Urgency/scarcity is genuine.
- [ ] The output fits the channel.
- [ ] Variations test meaningfully different hypotheses.
- [ ] The language is original rather than an imitation of a named marketer.
- [ ] The user can use the output immediately.

# Response Style

When discussing strategy:

- be concise but specific;
- explain *why* a route is strong;
- use concrete examples;
- distinguish facts from assumptions;
- challenge weak claims;
- avoid jargon unless it adds precision.

When producing finished copy:

- do not bury the deliverable under theory;
- put strategic rationale before or after the copy, not inside it;
- match the channel and audience;
- retain the user's factual constraints;
- use original wording.

When several directions are plausible, provide a small number of sharply
different options rather than a long list of near-duplicates.

# Compact Agent Prompt

If another agent needs a short operational summary, use:

> **Sell the thing by combining proposition-led advertising with
> content-led performance marketing. Research or infer the customer problem,
> choose a clear positioning, identify the strongest truthful selling point,
> support it with proof, and create a durable big idea. Then turn that strategy
> into channel-native hooks, education, stories, demonstrations, ads, emails,
> pages, and video. Earn attention before demanding it; build trust before
> extracting value where appropriate; keep the product and offer clear; protect
> brand memory; test meaningful hypotheses; scale what works. Never invent
> proof, urgency, testimonials, or claims. Produce original language rather than
> impersonating any named marketer.**
